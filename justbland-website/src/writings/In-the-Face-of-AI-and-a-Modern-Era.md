## Introduction

The rise of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and its effects continue to encourage more and more opinions about itself. Some propose that AI is the greatest advancement in human history, while others signal the death of independent thought. Those skeptical of the technology reject it and urge others to do the same. Praisers of its many benefits believe it should be involved, at least to some degree, in everything. As an undergrad, I see video after video of someone discussing the next "big thing" in AI; or maybe it's a recording of some commencement speaker being relentlessly booed by a crowd of fresh graduates for suggesting that "AI is the future." Why so much hate toward AI? So much polarity between differing opinions? There is definitely no shortage of them. And, as a consequence of the Internet, there is nothing preventing anyone from sharing their own!

I, of course, am no exception to the spread of opinions and ideas. My goal in writing is not to throw my own thoughts into the ring hoping all others will fall. Instead, I recognize a more personal need to solidify my *own* mindset. I am still an undergrad. I am still learning, still processing new information and ideas. I am constantly experiencing new technologies and tools "vibe-coded" into existence. Most of my friends in mathematics are left questioning the value of their own effort in their field. "Why develop skills when Artificial Intelligence can do everything I can, and better?" This question is not limited to mathematics and has seen many variations in art, computer science, recreational ventures, and hobbies. The question itself isn't entirely realistic (AI can not do *everything* a human can, at least, not yet); instead, I would claim that this question is a manifestation of major feelings associated with an evolving workplace. This isn't to disqualify other opinions, but highlight the personal need for a grounded view and the overarching need for more data on AI and its effects.

## The Reality

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (USBLS) annually updates occupational "job outlooks," a rough estimate of how much an occupation's total jobs will grow or decrease. The USBLS projects an increase of 10% over the next ten years for software developers as of this writing[^1]. In past years, this estimate has been up to 25%! So why include it here if the trend appears to be decreasing?

Those backing artificial intelligence signal major reduction in human involvement in certain occupations. A fair share of these people target software engineering and adjacent fields because of AI's capability concerning code production. However, "software engineering" involves much more than writing code. Hard skills required for the job such as problem solving, engineering judgement, and decision making based on data analytics are not easily accomplished without a capable engineer. Therefore, we can see how the occupation itself is not under threat of shutting down. Conversely, computer programmers are described by the USBLS as those who "write, modify, and test code and scripts that allow computer software and applications to function properly." The current job outlook for this position is -7% over the next 10 years, with the USBLS claiming that this work is subject to time and money saving measures[^2].

Two Harvard researchers, Fiona Chen and James Stratton, recently published a paper analyzing possible productivity benefits from AI assistants and agents in the workplace. According to quantitative evidence provided by their research, they "find small positive, but statistically insignificant, effects... following adoption of AI assistants and AI agents" (p. 3). When examining the current effects AI may have on employment, the researchers "do not find significant effects of either AI agents or AI assistants on junior or senior workers" (pg. 21)[^3]. The evidence produced by this study contrasts popular opinions purported across social media, such as those claiming an "end to SWE" or "significant reduction and/or strain in junior hiring." Staying grounded in current facts and reality helps protect from the pessimistic and possibly cynical opinions shouted from the rafters.

## People Were Always the Purpose

It is widely accepted that software as an industry will absolutely evolve. AI assistants and agents absolutely introduce significant code generation. These tools have and will continue to change the SWE workflow. While this may be an uncomfortable adjustment for most, it is still okay. The advancement of technology has happened throughout human history; it's how we progress as a human race. And, I would argue innovation ultimately benefits people. Progress in medicine improves health, progress in industry improves the economy and costs, and progress in technology creates opportunity. At the end of the pipeline, people benefit.

Notice how I'm broadening the scope. I'm doing this intentionally because I don't believe AI will effect one particular industry way more than it does others; it will have rippling effects through all industries. YouTuber Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown) posted a video titled, "Theres never been a better time to go into math"[^5]. He approaches AI optimistically and addresses student angst by approaching the issue differently:

> ...for a while I've thought that perhaps too many student go and pursue PhDs for the wrong reasons. You know, it's very comfortable to continue with some form of school if you've always liked school and been good at it, and PhD programs kind of feel like an extension of that. Whereas what you should be asking is **how do you want to add value to other people in the world and how can you use and develop your own skill sets to better do that?**

This question resonates with me. At some point in my academic career, goals, and aspirations, I feel like I lost sight of offering a service to *people.* Sure, the acquisition of skills satisfy 11-year-old me, but are they that valuable if I don't improve some aspect of someone's life? This may not be a goal for every student, but hearing Grant's question reminded me of a major contributor of purpose and value to my own work. And consequently, I find this concept to be a huge motivator today! Keeping other people in mind is necessary part of a forward-thinking mindset.

## A Tool With Little Restriction

Large-Language Model (LLM) chatbots introduced an incredible level of convenience for anyone with internet access. Custom images can be generated, personal advice can be received, school assignments can be completed, and relationships can be built, all using chatbots. Finding the solution to most problems can be assisted or outright solved by LLMs. Today, there are few limits preventing the average user from offloading some or all of their workload onto an AI.

This is documented by researchers Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave in their paper "Thinking—Fast, Slow, and Artificial: How AI is Reshaping Human Reasoning and the Rise of Cognitive Surrender"[^4]. The researchers call attention to "...a distinct phenomenon we call 'cognitive surrender': the decision-maker no longer constructs an answer, but adopts one generated by an external system" (pg. 4). If not used wisely, AI can create an illusion of competence in the mind of the user. This is especially dangerous for students needing to work through problems designed to present an intellectual challenge. Their own research shows how decision-makers "...display inflated confidence even when wrong" (pg. 18).

Everyone should keep this in mind. Admittedly, I have not always imposed proper restrictions for myself, and realized this only after being unable to properly reason on an exam. How much greater is the ability to reason in one's own career? I would urge anyone using LLMs to adopt appropriate restrictions so they can benefit from the tool instead of being harmed by their own use of it. Little restriction doesn't make AI inherently bad; it's the thinking and effort we are willing to forfeit for the sake of convenience.

## Conclusion

For the benefit of others, those around me, and myself, I choose to look ahead optimistically. It is largely unrealistic to believe jobs within my industry will be filled by artificial intelligence or robots. My purpose for working is to provide for my family while positively impacting the lives of others. And AI, as a tool or assistant, is beneficial to the human mind when used wisely.

I am not surprised by the continued evolution of this industry. Adaptability was taught to me as a teenager discovering proper work ethic, and I am grateful for those who invested in me so I could work with honor and excellence. That's the point: the mindset behind work *can't* change much. The work itself might change a little bit with innovation, but nothing strips away or changes my own attitude toward it. I recognize change as something essential, not because I always want it to happen, but because the deepest skills I learn are the most valuable and most transferrable.
